1. Introduction & aims

Modern neural text-to-speech (TTS) no longer only “reads the words.” A growing set of APIs let you write stage directions into the script — emotion words, delivery cues, and non-verbal sounds such as laughs, sighs, gasps, or breaths — so a single generation pass can sound acted rather than flat.[1][2]

This report starts from a practical baseline: personal projects currently using Gemini TTS, which supports scene direction, voice/audio tags, noises, and about 30 prebuilt voices, but costs more than desired.[3][4] Recent alternatives of interest include xAI Grok TTS / flagship voices, Fish Audio S2.1, and Qwen-Audio-3.0-TTS, with ElevenLabs as the familiar premium foil.[5][6][7]

The document aims to:

  1. Define a strict filter: compare only APIs that support emotion tags and sound tags like laughs.
  2. Answer how many commercial hosted models/product lines currently meet that filter.
  3. Normalize published pricing so Gemini, Grok, Fish, Qwen, ElevenLabs, MiniMax, and Cartesia can be compared on cost.
  4. Check whether the newest releases (Grok, Fish S2.1, Qwen-Audio-3.0) actually ship tag support — or whether new releases are “dumb voices” again.
  5. Give a practical recommendation for cost-sensitive personal projects that refuse to give up laughs and emotion cues.
Quick verdict: As of late July 2026, about seven commercial hosted product lines clearly document both emotion-style control tags and laugh/non-verbal sound tags. The newest releases are not tag-free: Grok TTS, Fish S2.1 Pro, and Qwen-Audio-3.0-TTS all support inline laughs and emotion cues.[8][9][10] On list price, Fish S2.1 Pro (~$15 / 1M UTF-8 bytes) and Grok TTS ($15 / 1M characters) undercut Gemini 3.1 Flash TTS’s audio-token bill and crush ElevenLabs v3 ($100 / 1M characters).[11][12][13][14] Gemini remains cheaper than ElevenLabs; Fish’s free s2.1-pro-free tier is the standout for personal prototyping if fair-use limits are acceptable.[15]

3. Limitations & strength of evidence

Theoretical / market limitations

TTS quality is partly subjective: “sounds human” and “the laugh landed” are listener judgments, not single lab metrics. Vendor demos cherry-pick good takes; tag obedience varies by voice, language, and how crowded the script is with cues.[16] Pricing pages change without academic archival discipline — a July 2026 snapshot can be wrong by September.

Methodology limitations

  • This report relies on vendor documentation, pricing pages, launch blogs, and secondary roundups (including Artificial Analysis–cited Elo figures in press). It does not run a blind listening study or burn real API spend across every vendor.
  • Billing units differ (characters vs UTF-8 bytes vs audio tokens). Normalized “$ per 1M English characters” and “$ per audio minute” are estimates using published conversion assumptions (e.g., Google’s ~25 audio tokens/second).[13]
  • Some tag lists are incomplete in third-party mirrors (HTML stripping of angle-bracket wrapping tags). Prefer primary docs when integrating.
  • Open-source / self-hosted models (e.g., Orpheus-style speech-LLMs) are noted but not cost-ranked like hosted APIs, because GPU time dominates price.

Field maturity

Expressive neural TTS with inline performance tags is a young product category (roughly 2024–2026 for the current “audio tag” wave). Independent comparative science is thinner than for ASR word-error-rate. Treat Elo leaderboards and marketing “#1” claims as useful signals, not final truth.[17]

4. History of the fields that try to answer this

Speech synthesis has cycled through control paradigms for decades:

Timeline (simplified) 1990s–2000s Concatenative / formant TTS Control = pitch/duration params, limited expressivity ~2004–2015 SSML (W3C) becomes the enterprise "tag language" <break>, <prosody>, <emphasis> — emotion often vendor-specific ~2016–2020 Neural WaveNet / Tacotron era Better naturalness; control often via style tokens / embeddings (not friendly inline script tags for hobbyists) ~2021–2024 Voice cloning APIs (ElevenLabs et al.) Emotion often "hope the model infers it" or separate emotion enums ~2024–2026 "Audio tags" / "speech tags" / "sound tags" product wave Bracket cues like [laughs] [whisper] [angry] written into the script Gemini 3.1, Eleven v3, Fish S2/S2.1, Grok TTS, Qwen-Audio-3.0, MiniMax 2.8

SSML taught the industry that markup belongs next to the words.[18] The 2025–2026 wave replaces much of that XML with natural-language brackets that models learned from training data — sometimes as free-form text (Fish S2.1) rather than a closed enum.[6] That is why “does the new model support tags?” became a buyer question overnight: without tags, expressive pipelines regress to regenerate-and-pray.

5. Inclusion filter: what “tags” means here

To match the user’s requirement — emotion tags and sound tags like laughs — a model must document both of the following in primary or close-secondary docs:

  1. Emotion / delivery tags — inline or SSML markers that steer mood or delivery (e.g., [angry], [excited], <emotion value="sad"/>, wrapping <whisper>…</whisper>).
  2. Non-verbal sound tags — cues that produce or strongly trigger vocal sounds such as laughs, chuckles, sighs, gasps, breaths, coughs (e.g., [laughs], [laugh], (laughs), [laughter]).
Excluded by design: APIs that only offer a separate “emotion” API parameter with no laugh/sound cues; APIs that only take a free-text “acting description” field without documented inline laugh tags (notably Hume Octave’s description-based acting instructions); plain TTS with no documented tag vocabulary (classic OpenAI TTS tiers without audio tags).[19]

8. Model-by-model catalog

8.1 Google Gemini TTS baseline 30 voices

Gemini TTS is the user’s current stack: prebuilt voice library of 30 voices, multi-speaker dialogue, natural-language Audio Profile / Scene / Director’s Notes, and — especially on Gemini 3.1 Flash TTS — a large vocabulary of inline audio tags (Google Cloud materials cite 200+).[1][3][4]

Common tags documented in the speech-generation guide include emotion and delivery markers plus non-verbals:

[laughs][giggles][sighs][gasp][cough] [whispers][excited][sarcastic][shouting]

Billing: not per character. Paid Gemini 3.1 Flash TTS is $1 / 1M text input tokens and $20 / 1M audio output tokens, with audio metered at 25 tokens per second (≈ $0.03 per second-minute of audio before text input).[13] Gemini 2.5 Flash TTS is half that on both sides ($0.50 / $10).[13] Batch API discounts apply on some surfaces.[28]

Best for: scene-level direction + multi-speaker scenes when you already live in Google’s ecosystem. Pain point: 3.1’s expressivity tier is the one that matches “tags + noises,” and it costs roughly 2× 2.5 Flash on audio tokens.

8.2 xAI Grok TTS / flagship voices new

xAI’s Text-to-Speech API converts text to audio with built-in and custom voices, streaming options, and explicit speech tags. Official docs show inline cues such as [pause], [laugh], and wrapping delivery tags such as <whisper>…</whisper>.[8] Telnyx’s mirror of the tag set lists laughs/chuckles/giggles, sighs/breaths, and wrapping volume/pitch/pace controls.[29]

On 2026-07-06, xAI announced 21 new flagship multilingual voices joining the original five (Ara, Eve, Leo, Rex, Sal), with speech tags called out for delivery control — about 26 built-in voices in the marketing lineup, plus cloning from ~1 minute of audio.[5]

Billing: $15.00 per 1 million characters on the Voice pricing table.[12][30] That is roughly half Gemini 3.1’s effective character-equivalent cost and about 1/6.7 of ElevenLabs v3 list price.

Caveat: Tag vocabulary looks smaller/more fixed than Gemini’s 200+ or Fish’s open-domain brackets. Scene-level “director worldbuilding” is weaker than Gemini’s Scene + Audio Profile stack. Still clearly meets emotion + laugh requirements.

8.3 Fish Audio S2.1 Pro new cost leader

Fish positions S2.1-Pro as the production model: ~100 ms time-to-first-audio claims, 83 languages, multi-speaker, and open-domain bracket tags learned as natural language — not a closed enum — e.g. [whispers sweetly], [laughing nervously].[6][31] Emotion docs enumerate laughs, chuckles, sobs, sighs, audience laughter, and many emotion words.[9]

Billing: $15 / million UTF-8 bytes for s2.1-pro (and prior S2/S1 paid strings); s2.1-pro-free is $0 under fair-use, without TTFA/DPA guarantees, with free-window messaging evolving through mid/late 2026.[11][15]

Best for: personal projects that want Gemini-like bracket directionality at Fish/Grok price points — especially while the free model string remains usable. Watch: fair-use / commercial ARR clauses on the free tier; UTF-8 byte billing bites harder on non-Latin scripts.

8.4 Qwen-Audio-3.0-TTS new yes, tags

Yes — Qwen released a production audio TTS line. Qwen-Audio-3.0-TTS launched around 2026-07-20 as hosted Flash (~300 ms first-packet positioning) and Plus (quality) models on Alibaba Cloud Model Studio, without public weight downloads for this hosted line.[7][32]

Controllability is dual-track: free-style natural-language style instructions plus 86 fine-grained inline tags for localized emotion/style and non-verbal events (laughter, breathing, coughing, sighing, etc.).[10] Examples in launch materials include [angry], [giggles], [laughing], [gasp].[7]

Billing: widely reported at about $27.59–$27.60 per million characters via Model Studio — between Gemini 2.5 Flash’s effective rate and Gemini 3.1’s, and far below ElevenLabs/MiniMax HD.[17][25] Plus debuted at or near the top of Artificial Analysis Speech Arena coverage (~1,236 Elo in press).[17]

Trade-offs: slower throughput in some reports (~16 chars/sec for Plus), 16 languages (strong Chinese dialect coverage), hosted-only, and some streaming-mode caveats for tag support noted in secondary guides.[32]

8.5 ElevenLabs Eleven v3 premium

Eleven v3 is the tag-capable ElevenLabs tier: bracket audio tags for emotions ([curious], [crying]), delivery ([whispers], [shouts]), and human reactions ([laughs], [clears throat], [sighs]), plus dialogue APIs.[2][33]

Billing: Multilingual v2/v3 API list rate $0.10 per 1,000 characters (= $100 / 1M), with Flash/Turbo at $0.05 / 1K for the low-latency non-v3 path.[14] This is the expensive end of the tag club — and the reference that makes Gemini look “cheap” by comparison.

8.6 MiniMax Speech 2.8

Speech 2.8 adds Sound Tags injected in parentheses — (laughs), (chuckle), (sighs), (gasps), breathing and articulations — alongside emotion presets.[21] Official pay-as-you-go lists speech-2.8-turbo at $60 / 1M characters and speech-2.8-hd at $100 / 1M.[22]

Functionally in the club; commercially closer to ElevenLabs than to Fish/Grok/Gemini.

8.7 Cartesia Sonic

Sonic supports emotion via generation_config.emotion or SSML <emotion value="…"/>, and nonverbal laughter via inserting [laughter] in the transcript.[16][23] Emotion control is documented as beta/experimental for mid-utterance shifts. Pricing is credit-based (~1 credit per character); secondary guides place PAYG near ~$50 / 1M characters.[26][27]

Qualifies under the filter, but the laugh vocabulary is thinner than Gemini/Fish/Eleven/Qwen.

11. Conclusion

How many models do emotion + laugh/sound tags? Roughly seven commercial hosted product lines as of late July 2026 (Gemini TTS, Grok TTS, Fish Audio, Eleven v3, Qwen-Audio-3.0-TTS, MiniMax Speech 2.8, Cartesia Sonic). Counting every SKU pushes the number into the low teens; the set is still small.

Do the new releases support tags? Yes for Grok, Fish S2.1, and Qwen-Audio-3.0 — all three ship inline emotion/delivery control and laugh/non-verbal cues. That is the opposite of a tag regression.

Cost: Gemini remains cheaper than ElevenLabs’ $100/1M Multilingual v2/v3 rate. Against the newest peers, Gemini 3.1 is no longer the cheap expressive option: Fish (~$15/M bytes) and Grok ($15/M chars) undercut it while keeping tags; Qwen (~$28/M chars) lands near or below Gemini 3.1’s effective rate with arena-leading quality claims. For personal projects optimizing dollars, evaluate Fish s2.1-pro-free → paid Fish or Grok before paying Gemini 3.1 rates — and keep Gemini when scene direction + 30-voice multi-speaker workflows matter more than pennies.
