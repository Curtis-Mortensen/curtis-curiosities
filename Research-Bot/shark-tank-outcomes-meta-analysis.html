1. Introduction and Aims

ABC’s Shark Tank premiered in 2009 as the U.S. adaptation of Japan’s Dragons’ Den franchise. Viewers routinely ask a natural follow-up: after the handshake (or rejection), how do these businesses actually do? [1][2] The prompt behind this report guessed roughly 10 seasons × ~20 businesses and asked whether other people have already tracked outcomes — specifically success rates and breakout winners — rather than inventing a fresh company census.

Central thesis: Yes — many people have tracked this. Across independent and semi-independent meta-analyses, three layers of “success” must be kept separate: (1) on-air handshake rate (~42% in season 1 → ~56–68% by season 10 → ~70%+ later); (2) post-show deal closure (only about half of handshakes become real checks — Forbes and HumbleRise); and (3) business survival / breakout (roughly ~70–80% of aired companies still operating in some form years later per commercial trackers, but true “household name / nine-figure+” breakouts are a thin power-law tail — Bombas, Scrub Daddy, Everlywell, Ring, Poppi, and a few dozen others). The show’s marketing value (“Shark Tank Effect”) often matters as much as the shark’s check. [1][2][3][4][5]

This document does not re-scrape every company’s website. It maps the existing meta-literature: fan datasets (Halle Tecco / The Hustle), journalism surveys (Forbes 2016 and 2023), commercial outcome trackers (SharkTankInsights, HumbleRise, Sharkary, StartupBooted), and academic papers that treat the show as an angel-financing natural experiment. [1][6][7]

2. Limitations and Evidence Strength

Theoretical limitations (first)

  • “Success” is not one thing. Survival, profitability, founder wealth, shark ROI, cultural fame, and acquisition price can diverge. A zombie brand with a Shopify store still “counts” as alive; a profitable lifestyle business that never scaled may look like a “non-breakout.”
  • Selection, not magic. Aired companies already survived casting, producer filtering, and often years of operation. Comparing them to “average startups” without adjusting for selection overstates the show’s causal power. [6][8]
  • Television incentives distort measurement. Producers favor drama; founders sign NDAs; sharks perform for cameras; some contestants admit they sought airtime more than capital. [3]
  • Right-censoring. Recent seasons look “more successful” partly because they have not had time to fail. SharkTankInsights’ near-100% rates for seasons 15–16 are almost certainly time artifacts. [5]
  • Private-company opacity. Lifetime-sales figures for Bombas, Scrub Daddy, etc. are usually founder / press estimates, not audited public filings. Rankings disagree across sites. [9][10]

Methodological and literature limitations

  • Tracker disagreement on basic counts. The Hustle / Tecco dataset counts 895 pitches across seasons 1–10; SharkTankInsights’ season tables sum closer to ~727 for the same span; Sharkary’s season-by-season table also lands near ~895 for S1–10. Different inclusion rules (aired vs. unaired filmed pitches, multi-founder consolidations, update segments) produce different denominators. [1][5][11]
  • Survey non-response. Forbes 2023 reached only 112 of ~300 deal companies in seasons 8–13. Non-responders may systematically differ (NDA fear, failure shame, success busyness). [3]
  • Commercial SEO blogs recycle each other. Sites like Mind Jumpers advertise a clean “94% success / 6% failure” figure that appears to refer to a narrower closed-deal subset (or older seasons) and should not be treated as a population rate for all aired companies. [12][8]
  • Academic samples are early-season heavy. Smith & Viceisza’s working paper covers through season 7 (N≈603). Chincarini et al. likewise work with earlier cohorts. Later-season equity compression and applicant quality shifts may change effect sizes. [6][7]

Evidence strength of this field

Outcome-tracking for reality-TV entrepreneurship sits between fan analytics, business journalism, and entrepreneurial-finance research. It is stronger than anecdote (multiple large-N pitch datasets exist) but weaker than clinical trial evidence: no randomized shark assignment, heavy selection into airtime, and noisy private outcome data. The best-supported claims are about deal process (handshakes ≠ closed deals) and exposure value; the weakest are precise ROI for sharks and exact lifetime-sales league tables. [2][7]

3. Precursor and Subset Questions

  1. How many companies actually aired in the first 10 seasons — is “~20 per season” roughly right?
  2. What share get an on-air deal vs. walk away empty-handed?
  3. Of handshake deals, what share close after due diligence?
  4. What share of aired companies (deal or not) are still operating years later?
  5. Does closing a shark deal improve survival beyond the “Shark Tank Effect” of exposure alone?
  6. Who are the breakout successes — and how many came from rejections?
  7. Do outcomes differ by shark, industry, gender, or season?
  8. How do Shark Tank survival rates compare to BLS small-business survival baselines?

4. History of Tracking Reality-TV Entrepreneurship

The underlying show format — entrepreneurs pitching wealthy investors on camera — began with Japan’s Tigers of Money / Dragons’ Den lineage before ABC launched the U.S. Shark Tank in 2009. [1] Early public curiosity was anecdotal (“Where are they now?” segments on the show itself). Around the mid-2010s, three parallel tracking traditions matured:

  1. Fan / angel datasets. Halle Tecco’s pitch-by-pitch spreadsheet became the backbone of The Hustle’s 10-season deep dive (895 pitches, 499 deals, $143.8M pledged). [1]
  2. Investigative journalism surveys. Forbes (Emily Canal, 2016) interviewed 237 of 319 season 1–7 deal recipients; Forbes (Jemima McEvoy, 2023) resurveyed seasons 8–13. These remain the gold standard for deal closure / term-change rates. [2][3]
  3. Academic entrepreneurial finance. Papers treat the show as a high-stakes pitch competition / angel market simulation — studying intention-to-fund effects on survival (Smith & Viceisza), whether sharks select outperformers (Chincarini, Cannice & Leary), first-impression competence cues (AEA conference work), and investor fame vs. survival (Becker-meets-Sharks). [6][7][13][14]

Commercial blogs (SharkTankInsights, HumbleRise, Sharkary, StartupBooted) then industrialized season-by-season dashboards. They are convenient and often careful, but they are not peer-reviewed and sometimes disagree on denominators. [5][4][11]

6. The Outcome Funnel

APPLICATIONS (~40,000+/year today) │ ▼ (<1% air) AIRED PITCHES ─────────────────────────────── ~900 in S1–10; ~1,400–1,500 by S16/17 │ ├── NO DEAL (~40–45% overall; ~58% in S1) │ └── still get "Shark Tank Effect" sales spike │ └── ON-AIR HANDSHAKE (~55–60% overall; rising to ~70%+ later) │ ├── DEAL FALLS THROUGH (~43–55% of handshakes) │ └── many still survive via exposure │ └── CLOSED INVESTMENT (~45–57% of handshakes; ~29% of all aired) │ ├── Still operating / acquired (majority) └── Shut down (minority) │ └── BREAKOUT TAIL Bombas, Scrub Daddy, Ring, Poppi, … (dozens, not hundreds)

HumbleRise’s crisp funnel for seasons 1–14: 1,274 aired → 766 handshakes (60.1%) → 368 confirmed closed deals → ~29% of all aired companies eventually closed a shark deal (with 65 deals unconfirmed). [4]

Forbes 2016 on non-closing deal companies

Among season 1–7 deal recipients whose deals fell through, Forbes reported about 87% still operating at the time of the survey — reinforcing that exposure alone sustains many businesses. [2]

Comparison to U.S. establishment survival (BLS)

Bureau of Labor Statistics business employment dynamics charts show roughly ~50–55% of new establishments still open at five years and roughly ~35% at ten years (cohorts vary by start year). [15] Shark Tank’s ~70%+ “still active” headline looks stronger — but aired companies are older, already-filtered businesses, not random new establishments. Selection likely explains much of the gap. [8][6]

Beware the “94% success” meme. Some blogs claim ~94% of Shark Tank businesses survive / only 6% fail. That figure appears to describe a closed-deal subset in certain seasons (StartupBooted cites ~6% failure among completed investments), not all ~1,400 aired companies. SharkTankInsights’ broader census (~28% closed/inactive) is the more honest population estimate. [12][8][5]

8. Deal Closure: Forbes, HumbleRise, and the Show’s Own Claims

This is the best-replicated finding in the entire literature: the televised handshake is not a closed investment.

SourceSampleFinding
Forbes (Canal, 2016) 237 of 319 S1–7 deal companies 43% said deal never closed; another 30% closed on changed terms; 73% did not get the exact TV deal

HumbleRise (2023 update) 766 S1–14 on-air deals 368 closed, 333 failed, 65 unknown → ~48% confirmed closure among known outcomes; ~29% of all aired companies close a deal

[2][3][4][5][6]

Why deals die after taping

  • Diligence finds revenue, IP, or margin claims that do not hold up. [3][4]
  • Sharks rewrite terms (more equity, loans instead of equity, control/veto clauses, redemption rights); founders walk. [3]
  • Founders get the “Shark Tank Effect,” raise better capital elsewhere, or decide they no longer need the shark. [2][4]
  • Some entrepreneurs admit they primarily wanted the commercial. [3]

If ~$220M+ has been pledged on screen across the show’s life, and roughly half of deals close, actual capital transferred is closer to ~$100–115M — still large, but far below headline pledge totals. [8]

9. What Academic Papers Found

Smith & Viceisza — “Bite me! ABC’s Shark Tank as a path to entrepreneurship”

Working paper constructing a dataset of all aired entrepreneurs through season 7 (N≈603). Using instrumental-variables and matching strategies, they find that the amount of an intention-to-fund (ITF) offer significantly predicts later firm existence and going-public outcomes, with limited effects on patents. Effects are heterogeneous by gender and race. Interpretation: ST funding appears to relax financial constraints for some entrepreneurs more than others; mentoring needs may differ by founder demographics. [6]

Chincarini, Cannice & Leary — “Angel Investor Impact: Clues from the Shark Tank”

Published in the Journal of Alternative Investments (2023). They find that many shark characteristics (experience, reputation, network) do not cleanly predict portfolio performance measured by survival and website traffic. Counter-intuitively, sharks do not, on average, appear able to select outperformers relative to companies that did not receive offers — but the reputational impact of appearing on the show as a venue is significant. [7] This academic result aligns with journalism’s “Shark Tank Effect” story: the platform often matters more than the particular shark’s stock-picking skill.

Other academic / conference strands

  • First impressions: AEA conference work (“Swimming with the Sharks”) links Mechanical-Turk ratings of entrepreneur competence/confidence in still photos to higher offer probability and lower post-show failure hazard among those who received offers. [13]
  • Investor fame: “Becker Meets Sharks” finds entrepreneurs do not clearly pay a celebrity premium in equity terms, but shark fame associates with higher project survival; excessive equity relinquishment associates with lower survival. [14]
Academic consensus (loose): Getting further in the Shark Tank process correlates with better survival, but it is hard to separate money, mentoring, selection, and publicity. The show-as-marketing-channel finding is robust; the “sharks are superior pickers” finding is not.

Rejection / no-deal hall of fame (compressed)

Meta-analyses repeatedly emphasize that some of the largest outcomes had no closed shark check: Ring, Kodiak Cakes, The Bouqs, and several others leveraged airtime, then raised elsewhere or scaled on cash flow. [3][10][9] That does not mean sharks add no value — Greiner’s QVC path for Scrub Daddy and Oza’s beverage rebrand for Poppi are counterexamples — but it does mean airtime is an independently valuable treatment.

12. Conclusion and Further Questions

Short answer to the prompt: Yes — lots of people have been curious, and they have tracked this. For the first ~10 seasons you asked about, meta-analyses say there were on the order of ~900 aired businesses (not ~200), about half to two-thirds got a televised deal, only about half of those deals actually closed, and roughly seven in ten aired companies are still operating in some form years later. True breakouts are rare but spectacular: Bombas, Scrub Daddy, Everlywell, Ring, Poppi, Cousins Maine Lobster, Kodiak Cakes, The Comfy, DUDE Wipes, and a few dozen others. Exposure often rivals the check.

What looks solid

  • Handshake ≠ closed deal (Forbes ×2, HumbleRise).
  • Majority survival among aired companies (~70%+) in commercial censuses.
  • Closed-deal companies survive at higher rates than no-deal companies in those same censuses — but causation is muddy.
  • Breakouts are a power-law tail; rejection does not preclude mega-outcomes.

What remains soft

  • Exact lifetime-sales league tables for private brands.
  • Shark-level ROI (distributed returns, not just survival).
  • Causal identification separating money vs. mentoring vs. publicity vs. selection.
  • Consistent denominators across trackers for “companies per season.”

Further research questions

  1. Can someone reconcile Tecco/Hustle, Sharkary, and SharkTankInsights into a single open, versioned pitch ID map?
  2. What is the distribution of revenue (not just binary survival) 5 and 10 years post-air — e.g., median vs. mean?
  3. Do guest-shark deals (Oza/Poppi) systematically differ in closure and scale from permanent-shark deals?
  4. How much of the survival premium survives a matched comparison to Kickstarter / trade-show peers who never aired?
  5. What is the founder wealth outcome (not brand survival) — including dilution, later raises, and acqui-hires?
  6. Has the post-season-8 applicant-quality shift changed the breakout rate per cohort, or only the handshake rate?
